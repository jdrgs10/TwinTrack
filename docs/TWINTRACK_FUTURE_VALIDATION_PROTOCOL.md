# TwinTrack: proposed prospective validation protocol

**Research proposal, 6 October 2026.** This proposal concerns the frozen academic predictor **V2, version 2.0-final.1**. It authorises neither recruitment nor data collection, and does not change the app or model. Operational definitions, approvals, sample planning and an analysis plan must be finalised before recruitment. Its primary question is whether this exact model predicts a future assessment more accurately than carrying forward the previous same-subject score.

## 1. Population, outcome and immutable forecasting procedure

Recruit adolescents from the intended school population, documenting grades, curricula, subjects, recruitment routes and exclusions. The outcome is the percentage score on **one identified, same-subject written assessment occurring 1–7 days after forecasting**. It is not term GPA, general ability or a composite of unrelated assessments. Obtain the assessment identifier, date, maximum marks and teacher-verified awarded marks through an agreed school process. Convert marks to percentages consistently. Record assessment type and shared paper/session; percentages do not make papers of different difficulty interchangeable.

Eligibility requires all seven inputs to satisfy the frozen input contract and a previous same-subject written-assessment result dated **1–90 days before forecasting**, already known at that time. Select the most recent eligible prior result, not whichever makes the forecast closest. Archive a data dictionary specifying each input's units, aggregation window, permitted source, measurement timestamp, freshness limits and allowed range exactly as implemented. The RHR field must represent the required wearable resting-heart-rate value; average heart rate, HRV, recovery heart rate and inferred fitness are not substitutes.

Use **one prespecified primary forecast per student–assessment pair**: at a fixed daily study check-in, take the first eligible complete forecast within the seven-day window, stopping at one day before the assessment. Lock this rule before recruitment. Preserve the forecast and its timestamp before the outcome exists. Later entries or WhatIf scenarios cannot replace it. If rescheduling puts the eventual assessment outside the required horizon, retain the record in the coverage flow and classify it according to a prespecified exclusion rule; do not choose another forecast retrospectively. Other horizons are secondary and remain grouped by assessment.

Before the first forecast, archive the model artifact, version, checksum, feature order, coefficients, intercept, preprocessing, clipping and rounding rules. Apply this exact inference path throughout. No outcome-informed recalibration, tuning, coefficient updates or personal training belongs to the primary frozen-model evaluation. Store sufficient provenance to reproduce each prediction. For a future approved study, use an independently governed shadow evaluation of the frozen inference function, with forecasts concealed until outcomes are recorded, to reduce feedback on the behavior being predicted. This is a proposed research workflow, not an existing app feature or permission to change production behavior now.

## 2. A feasibility pilot and a stronger validation study

A practical initial pilot could involve **30–50 students over 8–12 weeks with approximately 3–6 eligible assessments each**. These are illustrative logistical quantities, not a power calculation or a claim that accuracy can be established. Assess consent and retention, seven-input completion, measurement consistency, correct forecast timing, outcome retrieval, burden, and acceptability. Report exploratory accuracy with its uncertainty; a small school pilot cannot establish transportability, subgroup fairness or a stable individual model. Define feasibility progression thresholds with the school before starting.

The stronger study should prospectively recruit across several schools, with recruitment and assessment coverage broad enough for the intended use. Specify the target populations separately: future assessments in participating schools, previously unseen students in similar schools, and students in entirely new schools. One result should not silently stand for all three.

Plan size around precision, adapting [Riley et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-074821) and [Archer et al. (2021; online 2020)](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.8766). These clinical prediction methods motivate explicit precision targets for continuous-outcome performance and calibration; they do not supply a universal educational sample size. Set acceptable confidence-interval widths for primary MAE and paired improvement, bias and calibration slope. Specify plausible outcome variability, error distributions, school/student/assessment correlations, forecast counts, missingness and attrition. Use sensitivity scenarios and simulation appropriate to this clustered design, then recruit for the most demanding essential criterion. Do not apply independent-row formulas to repeated assessments without adjustment or borrow synthetic validation performance as a reliable real-world planning estimate. Publish assumptions and achieved precision. Precision requirements for subgroups may exceed those for the overall cohort.

## 3. Evaluation sets and leakage prevention

The frozen model requires no new training set. Its prospective test cohort must be independent of any real observations previously used to select or adjust this version. Newly fitted comparators require a separate development block; interval calibration requires another reserved block. Fix the allocation and calendar cutoffs before inspecting test outcomes. If an adequately separate development sample is unavailable, report frozen V2 versus persistence first and defer fitted comparisons rather than fit them on the test cohort.

For the **unseen-student estimand**, keep every record from each test student out of comparator training and tuning; use later prospective test observations. A prior score may still be supplied as an eligible input: “unseen” means unseen during fitting, not without educational history. For **new-school transportability**, hold whole schools out. School identifiers or school outcome averages from the test set cannot inform predictions. Grouped cross-validation within development is useful for tuning, but does not replace prospective external evaluation.

For the distinct **future-known-student estimand**, train on an earlier calendar block and evaluate later assessments from those students. Earlier known outcomes may be used only under the explicitly registered historical-data policy. Respect the time cutoff for every derived feature and any within-person baseline. Report this result separately from unseen-student performance. Random row splitting would mix student histories and often shared assessments across sets, answering neither question cleanly.

Fit scaling, transformations, imputation if permitted for a separate challenger, feature selection and hyperparameters solely within development training folds. Inner validation must preserve student grouping and temporal order appropriate to the estimand. No full-dataset normalisation, future outcomes, post-assessment behaviour, test-derived subject averages or duplicate assessment labels may enter fitting. The frozen model retains its own archived preprocessing unchanged. Retain an auditable split manifest and exclusion log.

## 4. Prespecified comparison ladder and ablations

Evaluate the following on the same eligible, held-out assessment events:

1. **Training-only mean:** the outcome mean from the comparator development data, carried unchanged into testing. Never use the test mean to construct this forecast.
2. **Uncalibrated previous-score persistence:** predicted percentage equals the eligible prior same-subject percentage, without fitted correction. This is the critical primary benchmark.
3. **Prior-score-only fitted regression:** intercept and prior-score coefficient estimated on development data.
4. **Prior score + study time + screen time:** a prespecified regression using those three features, fitted on the same development records.
5. **Frozen full seven-input V2 2.0-final.1:** evaluated without refitting.
6. **Full refitted real-data Ridge challenger:** all seven inputs, with its own training-only scaling and regularisation selection. This is a newly developed model, **different from frozen V2**.

Keep the fitted comparison family, tuning budget and prediction-range policy consistent and prespecified. Report any clipping, with unclipped challenger sensitivity results where informative. The primary contrast is frozen V2 minus persistence; comparisons with fitted models are secondary and must not redefine success after results are seen.

Prespecify the grouped ablations: **prior only; academic behavior only (study/screens); wearable only (sleep/steps/exercise/RHR); prior + academic; prior + wearable; full seven inputs**. Also remove each of sleep, steps, exercise, RHR, study, screen and prior score individually. These are secondary research models, with the same development rows, held-out events and tuning discipline; mark multiplicity and avoid selecting whichever contrast happens to be significant.

Within the future development block, a modest benchmark may compare OLS, Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net and one simple tree ensemble with matched tuning budgets. Neural networks are not a priority without substantially richer real outcomes or demonstrated benefit. This comparison answers an empirical question; no alternative is presumed superior or deployed.

To assess incremental predictive value, fit matched real-data Ridge models with and without each nominated feature, particularly RHR, using only development data and identical evaluation sets. Refit the remaining coefficients after removal. Setting an input to zero, deleting it from the existing seven-input interface or holding other coefficients fixed is a model perturbation, not a valid retrained ablation. A useful RHR ablation result would support incremental prediction in this setting conditional on the other measured inputs; it would not establish a causal effect, benefit from changing heart rate, or universal adolescent relevance.

## 5. Outcomes, uncertainty and interpretation

The primary endpoint is **mean absolute error (MAE), in percentage points**, with each eligible assessment event equally weighted. Also report student-weighted MAE as a sensitivity analysis so students contributing more assessments do not silently determine the conclusion. Secondary measures are RMSE, signed mean prediction error, observed-versus-predicted plots, calibration intercept and slope with uncertainty, and predictive R² defined as 1 − sum of squared errors / sum of squared deviations from the test outcome mean. Retain negative R²; it signals worse squared-error prediction than that reference. Report it as undefined if outcome variance is zero. A correlation or squared correlation is not a replacement for predictive R².

For each matched event, calculate absolute error for every model. Define **ΔMAE = MAE(V2) − MAE(persistence)**, so negative values favour V2. Construct paired 95% confidence intervals preserving within-student dependence and shared school/assessment structure. The analysis plan should choose and simulate-check a suitable multilevel or multiway cluster procedure; do not bootstrap rows independently. With enough schools, resampling whole schools preserves their internal dependence. With few schools, school-population uncertainty cannot be estimated reliably: label conditional analyses and generalisation limits explicitly, rather than treat a student-only interval as new-school evidence.

Before outcomes are inspected, agree a practically meaningful improvement margin, δ percentage points, with educators and students, considering score reliability, burden and intended decisions. There is no established universal δ here. A stringent success criterion is an upper confidence bound for ΔMAE below −δ, alongside acceptable calibration and coverage. An interval spanning zero or the meaningful margin yields an inconclusive finding, not proof of equivalence. Publish all prespecified comparators and distinguish confirmatory from exploratory analyses.

Report the full participant/assessment flow: approached, consented, eligible, complete forecasts, correctly timed forecasts, available outcomes and analysed events. Seven-input complete-case accuracy describes that selected subset. Report coverage against all otherwise eligible assessments and reasons for each missing input or outcome. Compare measured characteristics of included and excluded records where consent permits; investigate selection in sensitivity analyses without assuming missingness is random. Persistence may cover additional events: report its broader coverage separately and retain identical-event comparisons for accuracy.

Prespecify a small set of relevant subgroup and setting summaries, such as subject, grade, school and input source. If justified by the fairness question, ethically approved and voluntarily supplied, also assess age/sex and a minimal resource/access indicator; do not infer sensitive traits or collect them merely to improve forecasts. Show counts, error estimates and intervals when defensible. Small or sparse subgroups receive an explicit “insufficient evidence” statement; nonsignificant differences do not establish fairness. Examine forecast horizon, prior-score age and calendar drift without selecting favourable subsets.

Any future prediction interval requires calibration against real held-out outcomes. A displayed fixed ± value or synthetic residual range is not a validated student uncertainty interval. Reserve calibration data, freeze the interval procedure, then assess coverage and width prospectively. Standard conformal guarantees are marginal and assumption-dependent; repeated students, shared assessments and drift complicate exchangeability. They do not guarantee coverage for every student or subgroup. Dependence-aware methods and empirical checks would be required, and any such interval is a separately evaluated extension, not retroactive evidence for the frozen version. See [Barber et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1214/23-AOS2276) and the authors’ [conditional-coverage analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.04684).

## 6. Ethics, data minimisation and a separate user study

Obtain school and independent ethics review, age-appropriate assent and guardian consent where required, before recruitment. Participation must be voluntary, with clear withdrawal arrangements and no effect on grades, access to teaching or school standing. Explain that predictions are experimental. Define a safeguarding contact, distress response, data-access responsibilities, retention/deletion schedule and limits on withdrawal after irreversible aggregation. These proposed safeguards are not a claim of compliance with any particular jurisdiction’s law.

Collect only consented, pseudonymous research fields needed for the protocol: seven aggregate inputs, input/forecast dates and provenance, prior/result dates and percentages, essential school/subject/assessment grouping, eligibility and missingness flags. Retain the identity linkage separately under school control with restricted access. Avoid raw Health exports, continuous sensor traces, chats, photos, contacts, location and device identifiers. Document secure transfer, storage and access; minimise granular timestamps in released aggregates. Share analysis specifications and suitably protected aggregate results rather than identifiable student records.

A separate usability study may examine comprehension, task completion, perceived usefulness, appropriate trust, anxiety and misunderstanding of WhatIf outputs using mock examples or forecasts disclosed after assessments. Register questions, sampling and analysis. Positive satisfaction or trust ratings do not demonstrate accuracy, and accurate prediction does not demonstrate improved grades. Showing live forecasts creates an intervention and would require a separate design. Claims that TwinTrack improves learning or wellbeing ultimately require an appropriately controlled prospective impact study with predefined educational and adverse outcomes.

## 7. Future personalisation and reporting

The current academic predictor is global and frozen. Its personal input values and locally saved profile do not establish individual parameter learning; the separate wellbeing comparison’s personal RHR baseline does not personalise the academic coefficients. Future repeated-data research should separate within-student change from between-student differences and compare global, hierarchical partially pooled and individual models against persistence on future data. Determine sufficiency through learning curves, temporal validation and precision, not an arbitrary number of wearable days. Frequent measurements without frequent independent assessment outcomes cannot support complex personal academic models. See [Curran and Bauer (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.093008.100356).

Publish the frozen artifact identity, protocol deviations, split definitions, coverage, clustered estimates and all results, including null or worse-than-persistence findings. Adapt transparency principles from [TRIPOD+AI, Collins et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-078378), while identifying its clinical origin. This proposed study can establish bounded predictive performance and feasibility; causal recommendations, grade benefits, validated uncertainty and adaptive personalisation remain separate claims requiring their own evidence.
